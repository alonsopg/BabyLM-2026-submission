\section{Discussion}

The central question is whether compact memory can make small-data pretraining more efficient without changing the model or data. HHM tests this by reallocating a fixed prediction budget toward repeated discourse entities and online hard examples.

The completed local validation results do not support HHM as an improvement over random masking in its current form. The gap is small, but it is in the wrong direction: HHM ends at $3.0321$ mean validation loss versus $3.0023$ for random masking. By the training objective, HHM is therefore a controlled negative result rather than evidence for sample-efficiency gains.

The negative validation result is still informative because the diagnostics expose how the intervention behaves. HHM does not violate the fairness constraint: it masks the same number of tokens as the baseline. However, the realized mask mixture is substantially more random than the target mixture because heavy-hitter sources often lack enough eligible positions. This suggests that the current entity heuristic, error-key granularity, or mixture weights may be too conservative to improve MLM loss.

The official evaluation complicates the story in a useful way. Without retraining or changing checkpoints, HHM is slightly higher than the random baseline on each available official zero-shot metric: BLiMP, Supplement, Entity Tracking, COMPS, and both reading scores. These gains are not large enough to claim a robust win, especially given the entity-tracking variance, and the paired tests over three seeds do not show statistically significant differences. They nevertheless show that worse local MLM loss does not necessarily imply uniformly worse BabyLM behavior. The paper should therefore be read as a mixed result: bounded-sketch masking is mechanically viable and may nudge downstream linguistic diagnostics, but the current simple policy is not yet a convincing training-objective improvement.

The later adaptive masking pilots sharpen the next-step recommendation. Raw high-loss CMS-Morph changes some official diagnostics but worsens validation loss. Accuracy-Morph, which scores candidates by smoothed prediction correctness, is the only follow-up that improves matched validation loss while also producing a large Entity Tracking gain. The seed-2 replication strengthens this conclusion: across seeds 1 and 2, Accuracy-Morph improves matched validation loss by $-0.0155 \pm 0.0051$ and Entity Tracking by $+5.51 \pm 3.27$. It does not create a broad official-evaluation win, since Supplement is lower on average and COMPS is flat, but it is the only variant whose local objective and targeted diagnostic both move in the desired direction. The entity-augmented follow-up does not preserve this advantage, suggesting that repeated-token bonuses and late adaptive decay are not the right additions in their current form. If this line is continued, the most defensible path is to run a third Accuracy-Morph seed rather than to add more heuristic components.

Relative to the closest ACL Anthology work, the main difference is experimental scope. Prior adaptive-masking papers show that changing masked content, schedules, or predictability-aware token probabilities can improve pretraining or BabyLM performance \citep{lee2022efficient,yang2023learning,edman2025mask}. BabyLM system papers show that much larger changes, such as hybrid causal-masked objectives, lightweight architectures, curated data, optimizer changes, or masked diffusion, can be stronger overall routes to leaderboard performance \citep{hu2024babylm,charpentier2024gptbert,yu2024antlm,haller2025sample,kosmopoulou2025masked}. Our experiments are deliberately narrower. The value of the paper is not that HHM beats those systems; it does not. The value is that it isolates the masking-policy variable and documents which signals survive that constraint. Under this stricter test, raw loss and entity recurrence are not enough, while correctness-smoothed token and trigram sketches are a plausible low-cost direction.
